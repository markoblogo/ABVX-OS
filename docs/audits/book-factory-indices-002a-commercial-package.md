## Primary reader

Curious general nonfiction readers who enjoy surprising explanations of economics, data and everyday life, without needing economics, statistics or 1D3X knowledge.

## Reader-promise territories

### A — Strange measures

What the reader gets:

Ordinary objects can make enormous economic and social realities readable.

Strongest stories:

Big Mac, Starbucks Latte, iPhone work-time, cigarette-equivalent pollution, lipstick and underwear.

Commercial strength:

High curiosity, strong cover potential, easy to explain quickly.

Risk:

Can become trivia unless every example teaches a measurement rule.

### B — Hidden signals

What the reader gets:

Mundane products and physical flows can reveal changes before official numbers feel real.

Strongest stories:

Freight, Baltic Dry / container signals, electricity demand, reservations, Big Mac, consumer objects.

Commercial strength:

Feels useful and current.

Risk:

Can drift into investor/business forecasting if the tone becomes too technical.

### C — Measure / myth

What the reader gets:

Some strange indicators illuminate the world; others are memorable nonsense.

Strongest stories:

Lipstick, underwear, skyscraper, Goodhart/gaming, LIBOR/manipulation, AI benchmarks.

Commercial strength:

Adds skepticism, stakes and credibility.

Risk:

Weaker thumbnail hook if used as the main title frame.

- Goodreads economics/data-science shelf: https://www.goodreads.com/shelf/show/economics-data-science
- Current web title-collision searches for `The Cigarette Index`, `The Weird Index`, `The Underwear Index`, `Burgers, Lipstick & Underwear`, `Numbers You Can Feel`, `Signals in Strange Places`
- Current unconventional-indicator web examples from The Guardian, Money Magazine, Investopedia and RBC Direct Investing.

Findings:

1. Popular-economics comparables sell a concrete curiosity promise, not an abstract methodology promise.
2. `Freakonomics` and `Everybody Lies` pattern: title must signal surprise; subtitle clarifies subject.
3. `The Data Detective` and `How Charts Lie` pattern: reader wants help interpreting numbers without academic framing.
4. `The Cigarette Index` is memorable but collision-prone with tobacco, smoking, health and pollution shelves.
5. `The Underwear Index` is highly memorable but collision-prone with clothing/kids/novelty search noise.
6. `The Weird Index` has no strong exact book collision found, but is generic and noisy as a web/product phrase.
7. List/object titles are commercially useful because they communicate variety instantly.
8. Subtitle must include economy / economic signals / data meaning; vague readability lines are weaker.

## Single-object title risk

`The Cigarette Index`

- Risk: HIGH.
- Likely cold-shopper misclassification: smoking, tobacco industry, health, pollution.
- Mitigation: subtitle must rapidly broaden to burgers/lattes/strange measures; cover must avoid looking like anti-smoking/public-health nonfiction.

`The Underwear Index`

- Risk: HIGH.
- Likely cold-shopper misclassification: clothing, consumer behavior, novelty/kids/humor.
- Mitigation: not recommended as main title; can be a chapter/story hook.

## Broad-title risk

`The Weird Index`

- Risk: HIGH.
- Problems: generic, weak search identity, potentially sounds like a website/database/product, not a polished popular nonfiction book.
- It needs too much subtitle/cover work to become commercially clear.

## List-title assessment

`Burgers, Lipstick & Underwear`

Strengths:

- instantly curious;
- clearly broad;
- visually rich;
- communicates variety and human-scale economics;
- matches current web/media language around quirky economic indicators.

Weaknesses:

- can sound gimmicky;
- economics is invisible without subtitle;
- `underwear` can create novelty/kids search noise.

Assessment:

Strongest overall package if the subtitle includes `economy` and the cover presents the objects as signals, not jokes.

## Comparable positioning

Useful shelf neighborhood:

- If someone likes `Freakonomics`, this offers a similarly surprising lens, but focused on strange measures rather than hidden incentives.
- If someone likes `Everybody Lies`, this offers accessible data stories, but with physical/everyday indicators instead of internet search data.
- If someone likes `The Data Detective`, this offers practical skepticism about numbers through livelier examples.
- If someone likes `How Charts Lie`, this offers measurement-literacy through indices instead of charts.
- If someone likes `The Art of Statistics`, this is lighter and more object-led.

Do not claim bestseller equivalence.

## Ranking

### Rank 1

`Burgers, Lipstick & Underwear`

Why:

- stops the scroll through object contrast;
- correctly represents the book as broad, not single-topic;
- supports full-length popular nonfiction by letting chapters move across price, confidence, work-time, pollution, freight and measurement failure;
- has the strongest cover/thumbnail potential.

Main risk:

It can look gimmicky or insufficiently economic.

Mitigation:

Subtitle must include `economy`; cover should show objects connected by data/signal lines.

`Signals in Strange Places`

Why:

Clearer category promise and more serious shelf fit.

Main risk:

Less playful; may look like a business report without a vivid cover.

### Rank 3

`Numbers You Can Feel`

Why:

Strong conceptual promise and flexible scope.

Main risk:

Less concrete at thumbnail level.
