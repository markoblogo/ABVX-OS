## Comparable shelf

| Comparable | Useful market signal |
|---|---|
| `Freakonomics` | General readers buy surprising everyday-economics questions when the promise is accessible and story-led. |
| `Everybody Lies` | Data books sell when they reveal hidden human behavior through surprising evidence. |
| `The Signal and the Noise` | Forecasting/data books can be broader and more serious, but need a strong central thesis. |
| `The Data Detective` | Practical statistical literacy works when framed as curiosity, judgment and everyday sensemaking. |
| `How Charts Lie` | Visual/data-literacy books sell by warning readers about interpretation errors. |
| `The Art of Statistics` | Conceptual statistics for non-specialists works when examples are concrete. |
| Tufte catalog | Design/data readers respect craft and evidence, but Tufte is less mass-market/story-led. |
| `Super Crunchers` | Older data-driven-decision books show the appeal of quantitative counterintuitive cases. |
| `Priceless` | Value and pricing psychology overlaps with the reader-friendly index premise. |
| `More from Less` | Counterintuitive quantified stories can support a big claim when the narrative is strong. |
| Big Mac Index materials | The everyday-unit comparison is the clearest bridge from economics to unusual indices. |
| Ciggie Index | Strongest existing hook: pollution translated into a vivid everyday comparison. |

## Primary reader

Curious nonfiction readers who enjoy:

- strange but true comparisons;
- popular economics;
- data stories;
- hidden systems;
- practical skepticism about numbers;
- books that make abstract issues feel concrete.

This is not primarily for:

- quantitative finance professionals;
- academic economists;
- index-methodology specialists;
- 1D3X/POP/SPIKE users.

## Reader promise

Primary promise:

> You will learn how strange comparisons turn abstract systems into measures you can feel — and how to tell the useful ones from the misleading ones.

Secondary promises:

- understand why the Big Mac Index became memorable;
- see how similar comparisons can explain pollution, prices, purchasing power, risk and daily life;
- learn a lightweight checklist for judging whether an index is useful or deceptive;
- collect a small atlas of unusual indices worth remembering.

## Narrative architecture options

### Option A — Hook-first atlas

Start with the Ciggie Index, then expand into a curated atlas of strange comparisons.

Best for Amazon:

- strongest immediate hook;
- browseable;
- easy subtitle promise.

Risk:

- can become list-like unless each chapter has a clear interpretive lesson.

### Option B — Method-first popular economics

Start with why unusual indices work, then develop categories and case studies.

Best for authority:

- cleaner intellectual frame;
- stronger methodology.

Risk:

- weaker opening for mass-market readers.

### Option C — Build-your-own-index journey

Frame the book as teaching readers how to invent/evaluate useful indices.

Best for practical readers:

- clear utility;
- supports appendices and checklists.

Risk:

- may feel like a handbook rather than commercial narrative nonfiction.

Recommended:

Option A with Option C as the closing practical payoff.

### B — strong with fact-checking

- Waffle House-style operational signals.
- Fragility/resilience indices.
- Cost-of-living and wage-time comparisons.
- Environmental equivalents and risk translation.

### C — use selectively

- Owner project examples: POP, 1D3X, SPIKE.
- Technical index-building details.
- Internal ABVX/Cortex provenance.

### D — cut or defer

- Promotional passages for owner projects.
- Long methodology appendices before reader demand is proven.
- AI governance material disconnected from unusual-index promise.
- Raw normalized-corpus dumps.
